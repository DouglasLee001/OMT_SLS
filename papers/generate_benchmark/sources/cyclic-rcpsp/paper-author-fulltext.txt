L113: 1 Introduction
L114: 
L115: The cyclic scheduling problem concerns setting times for a set of activities, to
L116: 
L117: be indeﬁnitely repeated, subject to precedence and resource constraints. It can
L118: be found in many application areas. For instance, it arises in compiler design
L119: 
L120: implementing loops on parallel architecture, and on data-ﬂow computations in
L121: 
L122: embedded applications. Moreover, cyclic scheduling can be found in mass pro-
L123: 
L124: duction, such as cyclic shop or Hoist scheduling problems.
L125: 
L126: In cyclic scheduling often the notion of optimality is related to the period of
L127: 
L128: the schedule. A minimal period corresponds to the highest number of activities
L129: 
L130: carried out on average over a large time window.
L131: Optimal cyclic schedulers are lately in great demand, as streaming paradigms
L132: 
L133: are gaining momentum across a wide spectrum of computing platforms, ranging
L134: 
L135: from multi-media encoding and decoding in mobile and consumer devices, to
L136: 
L137: advanced packet processing in network appliances, to high-quality rendering in
L138: 
L139: 2 Alessio Bonﬁetti, Michele Lombardi, Luca Benini, and Michela Milano
L140: 
L141: game consoles. In stream computing, an application can be abstracted as a set
L142: of tasks that have to be performed on incoming items (frames) of a data stream.
L143: 
L144: A typical example is video decoding, where a compressed video stream has to
L145: 
L146: be expanded and rendered. As video compression exploits temporal correlation
L147: 
L148: between successive frames, decoding is not pure process-and-forward and com-
L149: 
L150: putation on the current frame depends on the previously decoded frame. These
L151: 
L152: dependencies must be taken into account in the scheduling model. In embedded
L153: computing contexts, resource constraints (computational units and buﬀer stor-
L154: 
L155: age) imposed by the underlying hardware platforms are of great importance. In
L156: 
L157: addition, the computational eﬀort which can be spent to compute an optimal
L158: 
L159: schedule is often limited by cost and time-to-market considerations.
L160: 
L161: In this paper we introduce a Constraint Programming approach based on
L162: 
L163: modular arithmetic for computing minimum-period resource-constrained cyclic
L164: schedules. Our main contribution is an original modular precedence constraint
L165: 
L166: and its ﬁltering algorithm. The solver has several interesting characteristics: it
L167: 
L168: deals eﬀectively with temporal and resource constraints, it computes very high
L169: 
L170: quality solutions in a short time, but it can also be pushed to run complete search.
L171: 
L172: An extensive experimental evaluation on a number of non-trivial synthetic in-
L173: 
L174: stances and on a set of realistic industrial instances (coming from a compiler
L175: for a high performance embedded processor) gave promising results compared
L176: 
L177: with state-of-the art ILP-based (Integer Linear Programming) schedulers which
L178: 
L179: perform iterative tightening of the modulus to converge to the optimal period.

L181: 2 The Problem
L182: 
L183: The cyclic scheduling problem is deﬁned on a directed graph G(V,A) with n
L184: 
L185: (|V|=n) nodes that represent activities with ﬁxed durations d i, and m(|A|=m)
L186: 
L187: arcs representing dependency between the activities.
L188: As the problem is periodic (it is executed an inﬁnite number of times) we have
L189: 
L190: an inﬁnite number of repetitions of the same task. We call start(i, ω) the starting
L191: 
L192: time of activity i at repetition ω. An edge (i, j) in this setting is interpreted
L193: 
L194: as a precedence constraint such that: start(j, ω)≥start(i, ω ) + d i. Moreover, a
L195: 
L196: dependency edge from activity i to activity j might be associated with a minimal
L197: 
L198: time lag θ(i,j)and a repetition distance δ(i,j ). Every edge of the graph can be
L199: formally represented as:
L200: 
L201: start(j, ω)≥start(i, ω −δ(i,j)) + d i+θ(i,j )(1)
L202: 
L203: In other words, the start time of j at iteration ω must be higher than the sum of
L204: 
L205: the time lag θ and the end time of i at ω shifted by the repetition distance δ of
L206: 
L207: the arc. For a periodic schedule, the start times follow a static pattern, repeated
L208: 
L209: over iterations: start(i, ω) = start(i, 0) + ω·λ, where λ > 0 is the duration of an
L210: 
L211: iteration (i.e. the iteration period, or modulus) and start(i, 0) is the start time
L212: of the ﬁrst execution. Hence, we can deduce that scheduling a periodic task-
L213: 
L214: set implies ﬁnding feasible assignments for start(i, 0) and a feasible modulus λ.
L215: 
L216: As the start times at iteration 0 might be greater than the modulus, we can
L217:

L219: 
L220: decompose it as: start(i, 0) = s i+k i·λ, where s i is the start time within the
L221: 
L222: modulus (0 ≤s i≤λ−d i) and k i, called iteration number, refers to the number
L223: 
L224: of full periods elapsed before start(i, 0) is scheduled.
L225: The key step of our approach is the generalization of equation (1) that enables
L226: 
L227: activities to be assigned to arbitrary iterations (i.e. by taking into account inter-
L228: 
L229: iteration overlappings). Hence, for every (i, j), we have a modular precedence
L230: 
L231: constraint:
L232: 
L233: s j+k j·λ≥s i+d i+θ(i,j)+ (k i−δ(i,j ))·λ(2)
L234: 
L235: where k i and k j denote the iteration numbers to which activities i and j belong.
L236: 
L237: Note that, by deﬁnition k j≥k i−δ(i,j). The modular precedence relation can be
L238: satisﬁed either by modiﬁcation of the start times s i, or by moving the involved
L239: 
L240: activities across iterations.
L241: 
L242: Finally, each activity i requires a certain amount req i,r k of one or more re-
L243: 
L244: newable resources r k with capacity cap r k.
L245: 
L246: The problem consists in ﬁnding a schedule (that is, an assignment of start
L247: 
L248: times s and iteration values k to activities), such that no resource capacity is
L249: 
L250: exceed at any point of time and the modulus λ of the schedule (that is the
L251: 
L252: makespan) is minimized.
L253: In the context of chemical processes, the problem described above is described
L254: 
L255: via Petri net’s (see [23]), where δ is the number of markers over the places. In
L256: 
L257: embedded system design this model is equivalent to scheduling homogeneous
L258: 
L259: synchronous data-ﬂow graphs (see [18]), where δ is the number of initial tokens
L260: 
L261: over the buﬀers.

L709: 3.3 Search
L710: 
L711: The solver is based on tree search adopting a schedule or postpone approach
L712: 
L713: (described in [17]).
L714: 
L715: The main idea underlying the search strategy is the following: since the sched-
L716: ule is periodic, the starting time values of each activity can be positioned with
L717: 
L718: respect to an arbitrarily chosen reference; we select one activity as reference and
L719: 
L720: assign its starting time to zero. The choice is guided by a simple heuristics; in
L721: 
L722: particular, the candidates to become the reference node should have no in-going
L723: 
L724: arc with a strictly positive δ. Formally, for node i to be a candidate, it must hold
L725: 
L726: @(j, i)∈A such that δ(j,i)>0. Then we accord preference to nodes with a high
L727: number of outgoing arcs. The rationale behind this choice is that it seems to
L728: 
L729: ease propagation of symmetry breaking constraints. Hence, the reference node
L730: 
L731: assumes standard values: t s
L732: 
L733: src = 0,k src = 0. Note that ﬁxing the start time of
L734: 
L735: one activity to 0 does not compromise completeness as the iteration variables
L736: 
L737: k∈Z can assume negative values: therefore optimality is guaranteed.
L738: 
L739: At each search step a new node is selected among the activities connected
L740: with the already considered nodes. Two activities are connected if there exists
L741: 
L742: al least an edge between them. This method improves the eﬃciency of the prop-
L743: 
L744: agation of symmetry breaking constraints. Other variable selection strategies
L745: 
L746: radically worsen the performance of the solver.
L747: 
L748: The search interleaves the assignment of start times and iteration values.
L749: 
L750: The algorithm assigns to an activity its earliest start time. In backtracking,
L751: the decision is postponed until its earliest start time has been removed. This
L752: 
L753: removal can occur as a result of a combination of search decisions and constraints
L754: 
L755: propagation. Hence, the algorithm, considering the same activity, assigns to it
L756: 
L757: an iteration value. Note that the iteration assigned is always the lowest absolute
L758: 
L759: value in the variable domain. In case of failure, the value is removed from the
L760: 
L761: domain and a higher number is assigned. In fact, if a solution results infeasible
L762: with k i=φ, it is trivial to prove that it is infeasible for any value φ 0≤φ
L763: 
L764: (remember that k sinkN ode ≥k sourceNode −δ).

L768: 4 Experimental Results
L769: 
L770: The main purpose of the experimental evaluation is to show our approach to
L771: 
L772: cyclic scheduling is viable; in particular, the focus is to assess the eﬀectiveness
L773: 
L774: on practically signiﬁcant benchmarks.
L775: 
L776: In this work we show four groups of experimental results: the ﬁrst (1) consid-
L777: ers an industrial set of 36 instruction scheduling instances for the ST200 proces-
L778: 
L779: sor by STmicroelectronics [2]. The second (2) group considers a set of synthetic
L780: 
L781: instances and compares the best solution obtained within 300 seconds and the
L782: 
L783: (ideal) lower bound of the instance. The third (3) set of experiments considers
L784: 
L785: a set of a synthetic instances and compares the solution quality of our modulo
L786: 
L787: approach with a classic blocked approach on cyclic scheduling (see [3]). Finally,
L788: the fourth (4) group of results is used to assess the eﬃciency of the combined
L789: 
L790: propagation of the buﬀer constraints and the symmetry breaking constraints.
L791: 
L792: The system described so far was implemented on top of ILOG Solver 6.7; all
L793: 
L794: tests were run on a 2.8GHz Core2Duo machine with 4GB of RAM. The synthetic
L795: 
L796: instances were built by means of a internally developed task-graph generator,
L797: 
L798: designed to produce graphs with realistic structure and parameters. Designing
L799: the generator itself was a non-trivial task, but it is out of the scope of this paper.

L801: 4.1 Industrial Instances
L802: 
L803: The ﬁrst set of 36 instances refers to compilers for VLIW architectures. These
L804: 
L805: instances belong to compilers when optimizing inner loops at instruction level
L806: 
L807: for very-long instruction word (VLIW) parallel processors. Since the resource
L808: 
L809: consumption is always unary, to obtain a more challenging set, in [2] the au-
L810: 
L811: thors replaced the original resource consumption with a random integer number

L813: 
L814: tion time is unary: the smallest instance features 10 nodes and 42 arcs, while the
L815: 
L816: largest one features 214 nodes and 1063 arcs. In [2] the authors present two ILP

L824:

L826: 
L827: the optimal solution for almost all the instances: our experiments compare the
L828: 
L829: optimal value and the solution found by our method within a 300 sec. time
L830: 
L831: limit. In addition, to empathize the quality of our solutions, we compare also
L832: 
L833: with a state of the art heuristic approach: the Swing Modulo Scheduling (SMS),
L834: presented in [20], used by the gcc compiler [13].

L1181: 5 Related Work
L1182: 
L1183: The static cyclic scheduling literature arises from two main contexts: the in-
L1184: 
L1185: dustrial and the computing contexts. The former includes mass production (i.e.
L1186: 
L1187: [15],[9]), chemical (i.e. [19]), robot ﬂow-shop (i.e. [6]) and hoist scheduling prob-
L1188: 
L1189: lems(i.e. [5]), the latter includes parallel processing(i.e. [21],[14]), software pipelin-
L1190: 
L1191: ing(i.e. [24]) and the emerging embedded system data-ﬂow problems (i.e. [16]).
L1192: 
L1193: There is a considerable body of work on the problem available in the OR
L1194: literature, while from the AI community, the problem has not received much
L1195: 
L1196: focus.
L1197: 
L1198: An important subset of cyclic scheduling problems is the modulo scheduling:
L1199: 
L1200: here, start times and modulus are required to assume integer values; as stated
L1201: 
L1202: in section 2 we make the same assumption.
L1203: 
L1204: Several heuristic and complete approaches have been proposed since many
L1205: 
L1206: years to solve this problem. An heuristic approach is described in [24], wherein
L1207: the algorithm, called iterative modulo scheduling, generates near-optimal sched-
L1208: 
L1209: ules. Another interesting heuristic approach, called SCAN and in part based
L1210: 
L1211: on the previous one, is presented in [4]. The latter method is based on an ILP
L1212: 
L1213: model. A state of the art incomplete method is Swing Modulo Scheduling ap-
L1214: 
L1215: proach, described in [20], [21], and currently adopted in the GCC compiler [13].
L1216: 
L1217: Heuristic approaches compute a schedule for a single iteration of the appli-
L1218: cation: the schedule is characterized by the value of the makespan (the horizon)
L1219: 
L1220: and by an initiation interval which deﬁnes the real throughput. However, the
L1221: 
L1222: horizon makespan could be extremely large, with implications on the size of
L1223: 
L1224: the model. Our model, instead, is compact, since both horizon and initiation
L1225: 
L1226: makespan coincide in the modulus value.
L1227: 
L1228: 14 Alessio Bonﬁetti, Michele Lombardi, Luca Benini, and Michela Milano
L1229: 
L1230: Advanced complete formulations were proposed in [11, 10], both report ILP
L1231: models; the ﬁrst comprises both binary and integer variables while the latter in-
L1232: 
L1233: cludes only binary variables. In [2] the authors report an excellent overview of the
L1234: 
L1235: state-of-the-art formulations (including Eichenberger and Dupont de Dinechin
L1236: 
L1237: models) and present a new formulation issued from Danzig-Wolfe Decomposi-
L1238: 
L1239: tion. Finally, good overviews of complete methods can be found in [14, 1].
L1240: 
L1241: To the best of our knowledge the state-of-the-art complete approaches are
L1242: based on iteratively solving the problem with a ﬁxed modulus. Modeling the
L1243: 
L1244: modulus as a decision variable yields non-linear mathematical programs; on the
L1245: 
L1246: other hand, with a ﬁxed value, the resulting problem is a linear mathematical
L1247: 
L1248: program. Hence, ﬁxing the modulus and iteratively solving an ILP model is a
L1249: 
L1250: common formulation for solving cyclic RCPSP.
L1251: 
L1252: Our methodology is constraint-based and tackles the non-linear problem as
L1253: 
L1254: a whole: the modulus value is inferred from the others variables avoiding the
L1255: iterative solving procedure thus increasing eﬃciency.
L1256: 
L1257: 6 Conclusions
L1258: 
L1259: We propose a constraint formulation based on modular arithmetic solving the
L1260: 
L1261: cyclic resource-constraint project scheduling problem. Keys of the eﬃciency are
L1262: 
L1263: three diﬀerent set of constraints: the buﬀer, the symmetry breaking and the
L1264: 
L1265: modular precedence constraints. In particular, for the latter we devise an original
L1266: 
L1267: ﬁltering algorithm.
L1268: 
L1269: The experiments highlight a good performance and a solution quality that
L1270: converges close to the optimal very quickly. In particular, the solver is extremely
L1271: 
L1272: eﬀective in ﬁnding a solution with a negligible optimality loss in terms of a few
L1273: 
L1274: seconds; conversely, the optimality proof takes much longer to complete.
L1275: 
L1276: As a natural extension, future works will focus on allowing an activity to
L1277: 
L1278: be scheduled across diﬀerent iterations. Consequently a new cumulative ﬁltering
L1279: 
L1280: algorithm should be studied.
